# Prompt Patterns

Numbered catalog of common prompt patterns, ordered by strength and frequency.
Each pattern has a "Watch for" section (what to look for) and a "Problem" section
(why it's a tell). Patterns are numbered from 1 without gaps.

A new pattern earns a number only when no existing pattern already implies it.
Prefer folding a new tell into an existing pattern.

---

## 1. Vague Quantifiers

**Watch for:** "several," "many," "some," "a few," "various," "numerous" —
especially when the text could give a specific count.

**Problem:** Vague quantifiers hide missing evidence. If you counted, say the
number. If you didn't, the claim is weaker than the prose implies.

---

## 2. Unspecified Authority

**Watch for:** "experts say," "studies show," "it is well known," "research
suggests" — without naming the expert, study, or research.

**Problem:** Unspecified authority is unfalsifiable. A reader cannot check
the claim, and the writer cannot be wrong. Name the source or drop the appeal.

---

## 3. Scope Creep by Adjective

**Watch for:** "comprehensive," "complete," "thorough," "exhaustive" —
especially when the text is a single document, not a systematic review.

**Problem:** These adjectives promise more than any single document can
deliver. They signal confidence, not coverage. State the actual scope.

---

## 4. False Precision

**Watch for:** "73.2%," "approximately 15.7," "nearly 8 out of 10" — numbers
with more precision than the source supports.

**Problem:** False precision implies measurement where there was estimation.
Match the precision to the source: "about 75%" not "73.2%."

---

## 5. Hedged Certainty

**Watch for:** "likely," "probably," "appears to," "seems to" — when the
source is definitive, or "clearly," "obviously," "undoubtedly" — when the
source is uncertain.

**Problem:** Hedged certainty cuts both ways. Over-hedging weakens a solid
claim; over-stating creates false confidence. Match the hedge to the evidence.

---

## 6. Citation Laundering

**Watch for:** A claim cited to a secondary source that itself cites the
primary source, presented as if the primary was read.

**Problem:** The writer is citing a summary of a summary. The claim may be
accurate, but the provenance is diluted. Cite the source you actually read.

---

## 7. Selective Comparison

**Watch for:** Comparing only the favorable results — "outperforms X" without
mentioning where X wins.

**Problem:** Selective comparison is misleading by omission. A fair comparison
names the trade-offs, not just the wins.

---

## 8. Temporal Vagueness

**Watch for:** "recently," "in recent years," "lately," "nowadays" — without
a date or time range.

**Problem:** Temporal vagueness makes the claim unverifiable. "Recently" in
2024 is different from "recently" in 2026. Give a date or a time range.

---

## 9. Category Error

**Watch for:** Treating a specific instance as a general rule — "this tool
failed, therefore all tools of this type fail."

**Problem:** A single data point is not a trend. State the sample size and
the boundary of the claim.

---

## 10. Inferred Attribution

**Watch for:** "The standard requires," "The code mandates," "Best practices
dictate" — without naming the specific standard, code, or authority.

**Problem:** Inferred attribution hides the source of the requirement. Name
the specific section, clause, or document that supports the claim.
